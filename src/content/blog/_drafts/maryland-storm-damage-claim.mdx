# Filing a Storm Damage Roof Claim in Maryland

Maryland's weather is genuinely varied and genuinely destructive. The Chesapeake Bay corridor funnels moisture that turns ordinary thunderstorms into hail events, and the state sits squarely in the track of nor'easters that can strip 30-year shingles in a single night. If your roof took a hit, the claims process has real teeth — Maryland insurance regulations give you specific rights that most homeowners never use because they don't know they exist.

Here's exactly how to move through a Maryland storm damage claim, what the law requires of your insurer, and where contractors are legally limited in what they can ask of you.

## Maryland's Storm Risk: What's Actually Hitting Your Roof

Baltimore and the surrounding counties average 3–5 significant hail events per year, based on NOAA storm event data from 2014–2024. Quarter-size hail (1 inch diameter) is enough to crack asphalt shingles and dent aluminum gutters. The Eastern Shore sees fewer hail events but higher wind speeds during nor'easters — sustained 50–60 mph gusts are common in Talbot and Dorchester counties during winter storm events.

Tropical systems are the third threat. Remnants of Atlantic hurricanes regularly track up the Bay, dropping 4–8 inches of rain over 24 hours. That kind of water volume exposes every failed seam, cracked flashing, and lifted shingle tab on your roof simultaneously.

The result: Maryland homeowners file more multi-peril residential claims per capita than most mid-Atlantic states. Knowing your insurer's obligations — and your rights — makes a material difference in what you recover.

## What Maryland Insurance Law Requires of Your Insurer

Maryland Insurance Code §19-103 sets the following minimum standards for residential property claims:

- **Acknowledgment of claim**: within 10 business days of filing
- **Acceptance or denial**: within 15 business days of receiving all requested documentation
- **Payment or denial of undisputed amounts**: promptly after coverage is established

If your insurer misses these windows, document every contact in writing. Maryland's Insurance Administration (MIA) at mdinsurance.state.md.us accepts consumer complaints, and a formal complaint frequently accelerates a stalled claim. The MIA also has authority to investigate claim-handling practices — it's not just a suggestion box.

## How to Document Your Damage Before Anyone Else Arrives

Walk your property from the ground within 24 hours of the storm. You're looking for:

- Missing, curled, or cracked shingles
- Granule loss concentrated at the gutter line (granules collect in gutters after hail)
- Dented or cracked ridge cap
- Damaged or bent gutters and downspouts
- Impact marks on soft metals — flashing, vent caps, HVAC equipment

Take time-stamped photos on your phone. Cross-reference the storm date against NOAA's storm event database at ncdc.noaa.gov to pull the official record — storm date, location, hail size, wind speed. Insurers use this data internally; you should have it too.

Do not climb onto the roof yourself. Beyond the obvious safety risk, an unaccompanied homeowner on a damaged roof creates liability questions if you fall, and your photos from the ground plus a licensed inspector's report carry more weight with an adjuster than shaky selfies taken from the peak.

## The 8-Step Claims Process for Maryland Homeowners

Follow these steps in order. Skipping steps — especially documentation before repairs — is the most common reason Maryland claims get underpaid.

1. **Document the damage immediately after the storm.** Walk your property from the ground and photograph every visible sign of damage — missing shingles, dented gutters, damaged fascia, debris impact marks. Do not climb onto the roof yourself. Take time-stamped photos and note the storm date. Cross-reference with NOAA's storm event database (ncdc.noaa.gov) to confirm the weather event, which strengthens your claim.

2. **Prevent further damage with temporary repairs.** Maryland insurance law requires policyholders to mitigate further damage after a loss. Cover any exposed deck areas with tarps and document everything you do — photos before, during, and after. Save all receipts. These costs are typically reimbursable under your policy's Additional Living Expenses or Coverage A provisions.

3. **Call your insurance company to open the claim.** Contact your insurer's claims line within 24–72 hours of the storm. Provide your policy number, the date of loss, and a general description of the damage. Ask for your claim number in writing and confirm the adjuster assignment timeline — Maryland insurers are required under MD Insurance Code §19-103 to acknowledge a claim within 10 business days.

4. **Get a licensed roofing contractor to inspect before the adjuster arrives.** Schedule a professional roof inspection before the insurance adjuster visits. A licensed roofer will document damage the adjuster might miss — granule loss, soft-metal denting on flashing, hidden deck damage. Maryland law does not prohibit a contractor from being present during the adjuster's inspection, and having an independent assessment in hand gives you a written basis to dispute a low estimate.

5. **Review the adjuster's estimate line by line.** When you receive the adjuster's scope of loss, compare it against your contractor's inspection report. Check for missing line items: drip edge replacement, ice-and-water shield code requirements, permit fees (required in most Maryland counties), and disposal costs. If the estimates diverge by more than 10%, request a re-inspection or invoke the appraisal clause in your policy.

6. **Understand Maryland's 1/3 deposit cap and 65+ election before signing a contract.** Maryland law (MD Code, Business Regulation §8-301 et seq.) caps contractor deposits on home improvement contracts at one-third of the total contract price. Contractors who collect more than one-third upfront are in violation of the Maryland Home Improvement Commission rules. Separately, homeowners age 65 or older have a right to cancel a home improvement contract within 3 business days of signing, regardless of any waiver. Read your contract carefully and do not sign anything that waives these rights.

7. **Receive your ACV check and schedule the work.** Most Maryland policies pay Actual Cash Value (ACV) first — replacement cost minus depreciation — and release the recoverable depreciation (RCV holdback) after the work is completed and invoiced. Endorse the ACV check, apply it toward your contractor's deposit (within the 1/3 cap), and schedule the work. Your contractor should pull the required county permit before any work begins.

8. **Submit your completion documentation to recover depreciation.** Once the roof is complete, submit the final contractor invoice, permit certificate, and any photos of completed work to your insurer. The insurer must release the RCV holdback within the timeframe specified in your policy — typically 30–180 days from the date of loss. Follow up in writing if payment is delayed beyond the policy terms.

## Maryland's 1/3 Deposit Cap: What It Means in Practice

This rule trips up more Maryland homeowners than any other part of the contract process. Under the Maryland Home Improvement Commission (MHIC) regulations, a contractor cannot legally collect a deposit exceeding one-third of the total contract price before work begins.

On a $15,000 roof replacement, that means a maximum $5,000 deposit — regardless of what your ACV check covers or what the contractor requests. If a roofer asks for half up front, that's not just aggressive — it's a violation of state law. You can report it to the MHIC at dllr.state.md.us.

This cap protects you if a contractor takes your deposit and disappears before starting, which is not theoretical — Maryland has prosecuted contractors for exactly this after hurricane season.

## The 65+ Cancellation Right: Know It Before You Sign

Maryland gives homeowners aged 65 and older a statutory 3-business-day right to cancel any home improvement contract, starting from the date of signing. This applies regardless of what the contract says. A contractor cannot legally require you to waive this right, and any clause attempting to do so is void under Maryland law.

If you're 65 or older and sign a roofing contract on a Monday, you have until Thursday to cancel without penalty. Put any cancellation in writing and send it certified mail.

This right exists because storm-chasing contractors have historically targeted older homeowners in the aftermath of major weather events. The post-storm pressure to sign quickly is real — this law is the legislature's direct response to it.

## ACV vs. RCV: Where Most Maryland Homeowners Leave Money Behind

Most Maryland homeowners policies pay replacement cost value (RCV) — what it actually costs to replace the damaged roof with new materials and labor at today's prices. But the first check you receive is almost always the ACV payment: the RCV minus depreciation.

On a 15-year-old 2,000 sq ft roof in Baltimore County, that depreciation haircut can be $4,000–$7,000. That money is not gone — it's held back until the work is completed and you submit documentation. If you pocket the ACV check and never replace the roof, you never see the RCV holdback.

The process: complete the work, get the final invoice and permit sign-off, submit to your insurer, and request release of the holdback. Do this in writing. If your insurer doesn't respond within the policy's stated timeframe, file a complaint with the Maryland Insurance Administration.

## What Maryland Building Code Requires for Roof Replacements

Most Maryland counties require a building permit for a full roof replacement, and inspections are common. This matters for insurance claims because code-required upgrades — items your existing roof didn't have but current code mandates — may not be covered under your basic policy.

Common code-required upgrades in Maryland:

- **Ice-and-water shield**: Required in Maryland under the International Residential Code as adopted by the state, along all eaves and in valleys
- **Drip edge**: Required along rakes and eaves under current IRC adoption
- **Ventilation upgrades**: If the existing attic ventilation doesn't meet current net free area requirements, a full replacement may trigger a required upgrade

Check whether your policy includes "Ordinance or Law" coverage — it pays for these code-required additions beyond the direct storm damage. Many standard homeowners policies exclude it unless you added it explicitly. If yours doesn't have it, factor the upgrade costs into your out-of-pocket estimate.

## Common Reasons Maryland Storm Claims Get Underpaid

The four most frequent gaps in Maryland roof claim settlements:

1. **Missing soft-metal damage** — Adjusters sometimes skip flashing, pipe boots, and drip edge, which are expensive to replace correctly
2. **No permit line item** — Permit fees in Baltimore City run $150–$400 for a residential reroof; Anne Arundel and Montgomery counties are similar; these should appear in every estimate
3. **Pre-existing damage exclusion overreach** — Insurers sometimes classify hail damage as pre-existing wear. An independent inspection report with dated storm records is the counter to this
4. **Depreciation never reclaimed** — The RCV holdback doesn't come automatically. You have to submit documentation and request it
