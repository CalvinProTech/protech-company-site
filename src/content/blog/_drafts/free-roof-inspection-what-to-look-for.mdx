# What a Real Free Roof Inspection Should Include (And Red Flags to Watch For)

After every major storm, roofing companies flood neighborhoods offering "free inspections." Most homeowners can't tell the difference between a contractor who will give them an honest assessment and one who will find damage whether or not it exists. Here's exactly what a legitimate inspection covers, what documentation you should walk away with, and which behaviors mean you should show the contractor the door.

## Why the "Free" Matters — and Why It Doesn't

A real free inspection costs the contractor 1-2 hours of a skilled technician's time. They offer it because a thorough, honest assessment builds trust — and trust generates repair or replacement business when it's genuinely needed. That's a fair exchange.

The scam version works differently. A contractor walks your yard, spots a few granules in the gutter, hands you a "damage report" that conveniently supports a full insurance claim, and pressures you to sign a direction-of-benefits agreement on the spot. You end up in an insurance dispute you didn't need and a contractor relationship you can't easily exit.

The difference between the two shows up in the details of what the inspector actually checks.

## The 7 Things a Thorough Inspection Covers

A complete inspection takes 60-90 minutes for an average 2,000 sq ft home. Anyone who finishes in 15 minutes walked around the yard and looked up. Here are the specific items a qualified inspector should cover.

### Step 1: Exterior Shingle Inspection

The inspector walks the roof surface — not just the ground — and checks for missing, cracked, curling, or blistering shingles. On asphalt shingles, they look for granule loss, which accelerates UV degradation. Any shingle that moves underfoot signals a fastener failure beneath.

### Step 2: Flashing and Sealant Check

Flashings — the metal strips sealing joints at chimneys, skylights, vents, and valleys — are the number-one source of leaks. The inspector probes each flashing for separation, rust, and failed sealant. Sealant that's chalky or cracked has lost elasticity and is no longer waterproof.

### Step 3: Gutter and Drainage Assessment

Clogged or sagging gutters force water back under the drip edge, rotting fascia boards and eventually the sheathing. The inspector checks for proper pitch (at least 1/16-inch drop per foot toward downspouts), secure attachment, and granule accumulation at the bottom — a direct indicator of shingle wear.

### Step 4: Soffit and Fascia Examination

Soft spots, paint bubbling, or staining on soffits and fascia boards are signs of moisture intrusion that often traces back to roof or gutter failures. The inspector presses on these surfaces; any give points to rot that compromises the roof's edge support.

### Step 5: Attic Inspection

An inspector who skips the attic is giving you an incomplete picture. Inside, they check for daylight penetrating the deck, dark staining on rafters or sheathing (mold or water trails), proper insulation depth (R-38 minimum in most U.S. climate zones per IECC 2021), and adequate ventilation — typically 1 sq ft of net free area per 150 sq ft of attic floor per IRC Section R806.

### Step 6: Ventilation and Ridge Cap Review

Ridge vents and soffit vents work together to exhaust heat and moisture. A blocked ridge vent can raise attic temps by 20-30°F, shortening shingle life by 3-5 years. The inspector checks that ventilation openings are unobstructed and that ridge cap shingles are properly nailed (4 nails minimum per IRC R905.2.6).

### Step 7: Written Report Delivery

A legitimate inspection ends with a written or digital report — photos of every deficiency, a prioritized repair list with rough cost ranges, and a clear statement of what is urgent versus what can wait 1-2 seasons. Any contractor who delivers only a verbal summary and a sales quote skipped the inspection.

## What You Can Check Yourself Before Anyone Arrives

You don't need to get on the roof to do meaningful pre-inspection homework. From the ground with binoculars, look for shingles that are raised at the corners, any visible gaps at the ridge, or dark streaking (algae, not necessarily damage). Inside your attic on a sunny day, turn off the lights and look for pinpoints of daylight. Check your attic hatch trim and any ceiling corners in upper-floor rooms for brown water stains — these are almost always active or past leak points.

Document everything with photos before the inspector arrives. If a contractor later claims new damage that you can prove wasn't there before their visit, that documentation matters.

## Red Flags That Signal a Dishonest Inspection

**They only inspected from the ground.** Ground-level checks miss fastener failures, improper overlap, and most flashing defects. A legitimate inspector gets on the roof.

**They found "storm damage" on a home in a ZIP code that had no significant weather in the past 12 months.** Cross-reference NOAA's storm event database (ncdc.noaa.gov/stormevents/) before signing anything.

**They push you to sign immediately.** A direction-of-benefits (AOB) agreement transfers your insurance claim rights to the contractor. In several states — Florida most notably — AOB abuse became so widespread that the legislature passed reforms in 2023 (HB 837) limiting the practice. Signing one before you've filed a claim or reviewed any estimates is never in your interest.

**No license number offered.** Every state ProTech serves requires roofers to hold a valid contractor's license. Ask for it. Verify it on your state's licensing board website before work begins.

**The quote is "only valid today."** Artificial urgency is a sales tactic, not a reflection of material prices. Roofing material costs fluctuate by 2-5% seasonally, not by the hour.

## How Often You Should Schedule an Inspection

Most roofing manufacturers — including GAF, Owens Corning, and CertainTeed — recommend professional inspections every 1-2 years on roofs under 15 years old, and annually on older systems. After any hail event with stones larger than 1 inch in diameter, or any wind event exceeding 60 mph, schedule an inspection within 30 days. Most homeowner's insurance policies require you to report storm damage "promptly," which courts have generally interpreted as 30-90 days depending on the state.

## What a Legitimate Estimate Should Look Like After the Inspection

If the inspector finds repair-worthy damage, the written estimate should itemize: linear feet of flashing to replace, square footage of shingles affected (a "square" = 100 sq ft), specific materials by brand and product line, and a projected labor timeline. A range is fine — "$650-$900 to replace valley flashing and re-seal chimney apron" tells you something. "Roof repairs: $TBD" tells you nothing.

For full replacements, get the estimate broken into material cost, labor, disposal fee, and permit fee separately. Permit fees vary by county and municipality — in most jurisdictions they run $150-$500 for a residential re-roof. Any contractor who says they'll "pull the permit later" or asks you to pull it yourself is trying to skip a step that protects you.
