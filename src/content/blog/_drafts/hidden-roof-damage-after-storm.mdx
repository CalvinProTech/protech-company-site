# Hidden Roof Damage After a Storm: 7 Signs Insurance Adjusters Miss

A storm rolls through overnight. Your yard is a mess, but your roof looks fine from the driveway. A week later you file a claim and an adjuster walks the roof for 20 minutes, photographs a few cracked shingles, and offers you $1,800. Six months after that, you've got active leaks in three places.

That's not a coincidence — it's a documentation failure. The damage was there. It just wasn't visible from the ground, and the adjuster either didn't know what to look for or didn't have time to find it. These seven signs are the ones most likely to be missed, and knowing them — before the adjuster arrives — is the difference between a partial payout and a full claim.

## Why Insurance Adjusters Miss Storm Damage

Most residential adjusters carry a heavy caseload after a major storm event. NOAA data shows that a single hail storm affecting a metropolitan area can generate 10,000–40,000 claims within 30 days. Adjusters working that volume spend an average of 20–35 minutes per roof. That's not enough time to identify functional damage that requires hands-on testing or systematic grid inspection.

There's also a distinction between "cosmetic" damage and "functional" damage in most carrier policies. Cosmetic damage — surface scuffs that don't affect the roof's weather resistance — is excluded from most policies written after 2012. Adjusters will often classify borderline items as cosmetic. Your job is to prove functional damage with documentation specific enough that it can't be reclassified.

## Sign 1: Lifted Shingle Seal Strips

Shingles bond to each other through a factory-applied asphalt sealant strip along the lower edge. That strip is heat-activated — it cures in warm weather and holds shingles flat against the roof deck. A wind event above the shingle's rated speed (typically 60–130 mph depending on the product) can break that bond without cracking or displacing the shingle at all.

A shingle with a broken seal looks completely normal from the ground, and even from a standing position on the roof. The only way to detect it is to press upward on the lower tab. If it lifts more than 1/2 inch without resistance, the seal is broken. Broken seals allow wind-driven rain to infiltrate the shingle layer, and they dramatically increase the risk of full shingle loss in the next wind event.

## Sign 2: Granule Loss Patterns vs. Normal Aging

Not all granule loss is storm damage. Granules shed gradually throughout a shingle's life, which is why adjusters will sometimes dismiss a granule-stripped area as "aging." The difference is in the pattern.

Hail impact creates discrete circular voids — 1/2 to 1.5 inches in diameter — where granules have been knocked loose from the impact point outward. Each void is centered on a dimple in the fiberglass mat. Normal aging produces even, diffuse granule loss with no center point. When you count more than 8 distinct impact voids per 100 square feet of roof surface, most carrier thresholds for functional damage are met. Document this with a measuring tape in the frame for scale.

## Sign 3: Granule Accumulation in Gutters and Downspouts

What comes off the roof ends up in the gutters. After a hail storm, the volume of granules in your gutters spikes dramatically. A cup or more of granules per 10-foot gutter section, combined with visible impact marks on the shingles above, is measurable corroboration that adjusters have a hard time dismissing.

Check the downspout splash zones in your mulch or soil as well. Granules deposit there and are visible for days after the storm. Photograph them with a ruler.

## Sign 4: Splatter Marks on Roof Vents and Soft Metals

Hail leaves distinct splatter-pattern dents on soft metals — aluminum ridge vents, lead pipe collars, copper flashing, and painted steel drip edge. These marks are diagnostic: the dent diameter corresponds to hail stone size, and the pattern across multiple surfaces confirms the storm trajectory. An adjuster who acknowledges hail damage to a vent but not to the shingles directly adjacent has a consistency problem in their report.

Photograph every soft metal surface on the roof individually, with a coin or ruler for scale. If your AC condenser fins are dented, photograph those too — they're not part of your roofing claim, but they corroborate the storm intensity.

## Sign 5: Attic Evidence — Daylight, Staining, and Wet Insulation

The attic is the most overlooked part of any storm damage inspection. An adjuster who doesn't enter the attic has missed half the evidence.

Enter the attic during daylight with all lights off. Pinpoints of light through the roof decking mean the felt underlayment and decking itself have been compromised — that's not just a shingle issue. Dark water-stain rings on rafters or decking indicate active or historical leakage. Wet or compressed insulation below those stains confirms water has penetrated to the living space.

Mark each stained rafter with painter's tape and photograph it with a timestamp. These photos, submitted with your claim, force the adjuster to include interior damage in the scope.

## Sign 6: Flashing Separation at Penetrations

Chimneys, skylights, plumbing vents, and HVAC curbs are all sealed with metal flashing. Storm uplift and thermal expansion during temperature swings open gaps in flashing without leaving any visible debris. Water enters through a 1/8-inch gap and travels horizontally along the decking before dripping down — which is why the interior stain often appears several feet from the actual entry point.

Press every piece of flashing flat against the roof substrate. It should lay with no spring-back. Any separation is a functional failure. Run a gloved finger along every flashing seam and look for daylight or flexibility that shouldn't be there.

## Sign 7: Bruising Beneath the Granule Surface

This one requires hands-on inspection and is the most commonly missed item in a standard adjuster walkthrough. Hail impact on an asphalt shingle compresses and fractures the fiberglass mat beneath the granule surface, even when the granules themselves stay in place. The result is a soft spot — sometimes called "bruising" — where the mat no longer provides tensile reinforcement.

To check: press the shingle with your thumb in an area with no visible damage. An unaffected shingle feels firm. A bruised shingle has a slight give, like pressing on a ripe fruit. Over time these bruised areas crack and split, creating leaks that appear months after the storm with no clear cause. Carriers are required to treat mat damage as functional damage under most state insurance regulations. Without documentation, they don't have to.

## How to Document Hidden Roof Damage for an Insurance Claim

Follow these steps before your adjuster's visit. The more specific your documentation, the harder each line item is to dismiss.

1. **Walk the perimeter and check gutters and downspouts.** After the storm passes, walk the full perimeter of your home. Look inside gutters for heavy granule accumulation — a cup or more per 10-foot section is a red flag. Check downspout splash zones for granule deposits in the soil. These are measurable proof of granule loss that a desk adjuster can't dispute.

2. **Photograph all ground-level damage before cleanup.** Before raking debris or clearing the yard, photograph everything in place — broken branches on the roof, dents in AC condenser fins, hail marks in window screens and painted wood trim. These peripheral items corroborate the storm's intensity and strengthen your roofing claim.

3. **Inspect the attic for daylight, water staining, and wet insulation.** Enter the attic with a flashlight during daylight hours. Look for pinpoints of light through the decking, dark water-stain rings on rafters, and insulation that feels compressed or wet. Mark any stained areas with painter's tape and photograph each one with a timestamp.

4. **Access the roof safely and check shingle seal strips.** If you can safely access a single-story section with a stable ladder, press the lower edge of several shingles. Factory seal strips are heat-activated — storm uplift can break the bond without cracking the shingle. A shingle that lifts more than 1/2 inch without resistance has a broken seal and is now vulnerable to wind infiltration.

5. **Look for granule loss patterns — not just volume.** Random granule loss from normal aging spreads evenly. Hail impact creates circular bare spots 1/2 to 1.5 inches in diameter, each centered on an impact point. Walk a grid pattern across the roof surface and count impact marks per 10-square-foot section. More than 8 per 100 sq ft typically meets carrier thresholds for functional damage.

6. **Check all roof penetrations and flashing.** Examine flashing around chimneys, skylights, plumbing vents, and HVAC curbs. Storm uplift and thermal cycling open flashing gaps that let water in without producing any visible exterior damage. Press metal flashing against the substrate — it should lay flat with no spring-back. Any separation is an entry point.

7. **Document everything in a written log and submit with your claim.** Create a written damage log: date of storm, storm type (hail, hurricane, tornado), each damage item, its location on the roof, and the corresponding photo file name. Submit this log alongside your photos when you file. Adjusters use standardized software (Xactimate) that requires line items — a log forces them to address each one individually.

## What to Do If Your Claim Was Underpaid

If an adjuster has already issued a scope-of-loss report that you believe is incomplete, you have options. Most states allow you to request a re-inspection with your own contractor present. A licensed roofing contractor can submit a competing damage assessment — called a "contractor supplement" — directly to the carrier. If the gap between estimates is significant, a public adjuster or appraisal process (available under most homeowner policies) can force a binding resolution.

Don't sign off on an adjuster's estimate until you have an independent contractor review it. A re-inspection costs you nothing. Signing a low estimate and then discovering additional damage later is expensive — most carriers will deny a second claim on the same storm event.
