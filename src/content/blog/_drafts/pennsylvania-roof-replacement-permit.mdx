# Pennsylvania Roof Replacement: Permits, Codes, and What to Expect

Pennsylvania homeowners often assume a roof replacement is a simple swap — old shingles off, new shingles on. In reality, the Pennsylvania Uniform Construction Code (PA UCC) classifies most full replacements as a "building permit" event, and skipping that permit creates real legal and financial exposure. Here's exactly what the code requires, what permits cost in the state's major cities, what an inspector is actually looking for, and what your rights are under Pennsylvania's contractor protection law.

## Does a Roof Replacement Require a Permit in Pennsylvania?

Under the PA UCC (Title 34, Chapter 403), any re-roofing project that replaces more than 25% of the roof area within a 12-month period requires a building permit. That threshold catches virtually every full replacement and most large-section repairs. Simple one-square patch jobs usually fall below the 25% line, but your local code enforcement officer — not your roofer — makes that call.

A handful of municipalities have adopted local amendments that are stricter than the statewide baseline. Philadelphia, for instance, operates under its own Building Construction and Occupancy Code (the "Philadelphia Building Code"), which enforces the same 25% trigger but adds wind-uplift documentation requirements tied to ASCE 7 load tables.

The practical takeaway: if your entire roof is being torn off and re-covered, pull the permit. No legitimate contractor should argue otherwise.

## PA UCC Roofing Requirements That Affect Your Project

The 2018 International Residential Code (IRC) is the base code Pennsylvania has adopted statewide, with Pennsylvania-specific amendments. Key provisions that directly affect a residential roof replacement:

**Underlayment.** IRC Section R905.2.3 requires one layer of ASTM D226 No. 15 felt (or equivalent synthetic) under asphalt shingles. Steeper slopes (4:12 and above) typically require one layer; lower slopes (2:12 to 4:12) require two layers of felt or a self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen sheet.

**Ice and water shield.** Pennsylvania's climate puts most of the state in IECC Climate Zones 5-6. IRC R905.2.7.1 mandates ice-barrier membrane from the eave edge up to 24 inches inside the exterior wall line — typically the first 3-6 feet of deck depending on eave overhang. In the Pocono highlands and other high-snow-load zones, local amendments sometimes extend that requirement further.

**Ventilation.** IRC R806 sets a 1:150 net free area ratio (reducible to 1:300 with balanced intake/exhaust). Inspectors routinely flag attic ventilation deficiencies during roof inspections, and if they find a problem, the permit can't close until it's corrected. This catches homeowners off guard — budget time for it.

**Drip edge.** IRC R905.2.8.5 requires metal drip edge at all eaves and rakes. It's a code requirement, not an upsell.

**Fastening.** Asphalt shingles in Pennsylvania must be fastened with at least four nails per shingle; in areas subject to wind speeds above 115 mph (per local jurisdiction maps), six nails per shingle is required.

## Permit Fees by Major City

Permit fees vary significantly by municipality because each jurisdiction sets its own fee schedule.

**Philadelphia.** The Department of Licenses and Inspections (L&I) charges a base fee plus a per-square-foot calculation. For a typical 2,000 sq ft footprint home, expect a roofing permit in Philadelphia to run **$200-$450** depending on project scope. Permits are pulled through the eCLIPSE online portal. Processing time for a standard residential re-roofing permit runs 5-10 business days for automated approval; complex projects can take longer.

**Pittsburgh.** The City of Pittsburgh charges a flat fee of **$100** for the permit application plus a valuation-based fee — typically 1.5% of declared project value. On a $12,000 re-roofing job, that works out to roughly **$280 total**. Permits are handled through the Bureau of Building Inspection (BBI).

**Allentown.** Allentown's Bureau of Building Standards and Safety uses a tiered fee schedule. A residential roofing permit for a standard single-family home typically runs **$75-$175**. Allentown requires permits be submitted at least 3 business days before work begins.

**Smaller municipalities.** Township fees across Montgomery, Chester, Bucks, and Lancaster counties generally run **$50-$200**, though some townships have adopted third-party code inspection services (companies like SAFEbuilt or EnerGov handle enforcement on contract), which can add processing time.

One important note: the licensed contractor should pull the permit, not the homeowner. When a homeowner pulls their own permit, they legally become the contractor of record and absorb liability for code compliance — including any defects found years later.

## What the Inspection Process Looks Like

In most Pennsylvania jurisdictions, a re-roofing permit triggers at least one inspection — sometimes two.

**Deck inspection (pre-cover).** The inspector visits after tear-off and deck preparation, before any underlayment or shingles go down. They're checking the structural sheathing for rot, delamination, or inadequate fastening. If damaged decking hasn't been replaced, this inspection fails and work stops.

**Final inspection.** After shingles, flashing, and trim are complete. The inspector checks that underlayment was installed (sometimes verified by a photo log your contractor provides), drip edge is present, ridge ventilation meets code, and flashing at penetrations (chimneys, skylights, pipes) is correctly integrated.

Some jurisdictions — Philadelphia L&I in particular — require photo documentation submitted through eCLIPSE at each stage rather than a physical inspector visit for standard projects. Your contractor should know which protocol applies and submit accordingly.

Inspections that fail add time. A failed deck inspection requiring sheathing replacement can add 3-5 days. Plan for it, especially on older homes in neighborhoods like Pittsburgh's South Side or Philadelphia's Germantown, where 1920s-era board sheathing often hides moisture damage.

## Understanding Your HICPA Rights

Pennsylvania's Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA, 73 P.S. §§ 517.1-517.20) applies to any residential contractor doing work over **$500**. Every roof replacement qualifies. Key rights you should know before signing anything:

**Written contract requirement.** HICPA requires a written contract for every covered project. It must include: contractor's name, address, phone number, and registration number; a description of the work; start and estimated completion dates; total price or a method to calculate total price; and payment schedule. An oral contract for a roof replacement isn't enforceable under Pennsylvania law.

**Right of rescission.** For contracts solicited at your home (which includes the vast majority of roofing sales calls), you have **three business days** to cancel without penalty under the Pennsylvania Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law (73 P.S. § 201-7). The contractor must give you two copies of a written notice of this right at signing. If they don't, your rescission period extends until they do — meaning you could technically cancel months later.

**Contractor registration.** Every contractor doing home improvement work in Pennsylvania must be registered with the Pennsylvania Attorney General's Office (PA HICPA registration number, searchable at attorneygeneral.gov). Working with an unregistered contractor voids much of your consumer protection and can complicate insurance claims. Always verify registration before signing.

**Liens.** Pennsylvania's Contractor and Subcontractor Payment Act (CASPA) gives subcontractors and material suppliers lien rights against your property. A general contractor who doesn't pay their shingle supplier can lien your home even if you paid the GC in full. Ask for lien waivers from subs and suppliers at project completion — a reputable contractor provides these routinely.

## How Long Does a Permitted Roof Replacement Take in Pennsylvania?

The permit adds time, but not as much as most homeowners fear. A realistic timeline for a fully permitted residential re-roofing project:

- **Permit application to approval:** 3-10 business days (automated eCLIPSE review in Philadelphia; manual review in many townships)
- **Material lead time:** 2-7 days for standard asphalt shingles; 3-6 weeks for specialty materials like metal or slate
- **Physical installation:** 1-3 days for a typical 2,000-2,500 sq ft home
- **Inspections:** 1-2 inspector visits; scheduling lags of 1-5 business days depending on municipality
- **Total realistic window:** 2-5 weeks from contract signing to closed permit

Projects that start without a permit and get red-tagged mid-installation — which does happen, particularly in Philadelphia where L&I is active — can extend by weeks and incur stop-work order fees.

## What to Look for in a Pennsylvania Roofing Contractor

Beyond HICPA registration, a qualified contractor in Pennsylvania should carry:

- General liability insurance (minimum $1M per occurrence for residential work)
- Workers' compensation insurance (required by Pennsylvania law for any contractor with employees)
- PA Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration number
- A physical Pennsylvania business address (not a P.O. box)

Manufacturer certifications — like GAF Master Elite status — matter because they unlock manufacturer-backed labor warranties that your homeowner's policy won't cover. GAF's Golden Pledge warranty, for example, covers both materials and labor for 25 years and is transferable if you sell the house. It's only available through Master Elite contractors.

For projects in the Philadelphia metro area, Bucks County, Montgomery County, or Delaware County, also confirm the contractor is familiar with local third-party inspection services and eCLIPSE submission requirements. Not every out-of-state contractor operating in PA knows the Philadelphia-specific workflow.
