# Florida Roof Replacement Permit Process: What Homeowners Need to Know

Pulling a building permit for a roof replacement in Florida isn't optional, and it isn't just paperwork. Under Florida Building Code Section 105.1, any roof replacement that covers more than 25% of the total roof area requires a permit. In practice, that means virtually every full roof replacement on a Florida home triggers a mandatory permit, inspection, and Certificate of Completion — regardless of whether you're in Orlando, Pensacola, or Miami.

Here's exactly how the process works, county by county, and what your contractor should handle versus what you need to keep track of yourself.

## Why Florida Roof Permits Exist — and Why They Actually Matter

Florida's permit requirement isn't bureaucratic friction. It's a direct response to hurricane loss data. After Hurricane Andrew (1992) destroyed over 25,000 homes in Dade County, post-storm analysis found that unpermitted or uninspected roofs failed at dramatically higher rates than permitted ones — even in identical wind conditions.

Today, Florida Building Code (8th Edition, adopted 2023) sets statewide minimums, and South Florida's High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) — covering Miami-Dade and Broward counties — layers on additional requirements: all roofing products must carry a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA), and installation must follow the NOA approval instructions exactly, not just standard manufacturer guidelines.

If your home is outside the HVHZ, you're still subject to minimum 130-mph wind design requirements in most coastal counties under ASCE 7-22.

Beyond code compliance, a permitted roof matters financially. Florida homeowners' insurance carriers routinely request permit records when a roof is over 10 years old. If you file a claim for storm damage and your most recent roof replacement was unpermitted, your insurer has grounds to deny or reduce the claim.

## What Your Contractor Handles vs. What You Need to Do

The licensed roofing contractor is responsible for the permit application, fee payment, on-site posting, and scheduling all inspections. You should never be asked to pull your own roof permit — Florida law requires permits be obtained by the licensed contractor performing the work, not the homeowner (with a narrow owner-builder exception that voids most insurance coverage and is rarely advisable).

Your responsibilities as the homeowner:

- Sign the permit application if required by the county (some jurisdictions require homeowner signature alongside the contractor's)
- Review the Notice of Commencement (NOC) before it's recorded — this document establishes your property as a construction site and protects you from lien claims; verify the contractor name, license number, and project description are accurate
- Request a copy of the approved permit before work begins
- Request and keep the Certificate of Completion after final inspection

If a contractor tells you a permit "isn't necessary" for a full roof replacement, walk away. That's either ignorance of state law or an intent to cut corners that will cost you significantly more in the long run.

## Step-by-Step: The Florida Roof Permit Process

### Step 1: Verify your contractor's license and insurance

Confirm the contractor holds a valid Florida State Certified Roofing Contractor license (CCC prefix) or a County Registered license valid in your jurisdiction. Check the license at the Florida DBPR website (myfloridalicense.com). An expired or mismatched license is the most common reason permits get rejected before submission.

### Step 2: Contractor submits permit application to the local building department

The licensed contractor submits a permit application to your county or municipal building department. Required documents typically include a completed application form, a signed contract or Notice of Commencement (NOC), roof product specifications (manufacturer spec sheets for shingles, underlayment, and fasteners), and a site plan or roof layout diagram. Miami-Dade and Broward counties require product approval numbers (NOA — Notice of Acceptance) for all materials used in the High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ).

### Step 3: Pay permit fees

Permit fees in Florida are calculated as a percentage of the project's declared value, typically 1%–3% of the contract amount. For a $12,000 roof replacement, expect fees between $120 and $360. Some counties add flat administrative fees of $25–$75 on top. Your contractor usually collects this at contract signing or submits payment directly and invoices you.

### Step 4: Await permit approval and post the permit on-site

Most Florida counties approve straightforward residential roofing permits in 3–10 business days. Miami-Dade and Broward can take 10–21 business days due to HVHZ plan review requirements. Once approved, the permit placard must be posted visibly on the property before work begins. Starting work before the permit is issued is a code violation under Florida Building Code Section 105.1 and can result in a stop-work order.

### Step 5: Schedule and pass rough-in inspection (if required)

Some counties require a mid-project inspection after the old roofing material is removed and before new material is installed. This lets the inspector verify the condition of the roof deck (sheathing), any required repairs, and that underlayment is installed correctly. Not all counties require this step — confirm with your contractor and building department before the project starts.

### Step 6: Complete the roof replacement

The contractor completes the installation according to the approved permit documents. In HVHZ counties, this means using only NOA-approved materials installed per the manufacturer's Miami-Dade approval instructions, which often specify nail patterns, fastener counts, and starter strip requirements more stringent than the rest of Florida.

### Step 7: Schedule and pass final inspection

After the roof is complete, the contractor calls for a final inspection. The inspector verifies installation matches the permit drawings, materials match the approved spec sheets, and flashing, drip edge, and ventilation meet Florida Building Code requirements. Most final inspections in Florida are completed within 2–5 business days of the request.

### Step 8: Receive Certificate of Completion

Once the final inspection passes, the building department issues a Certificate of Completion (CC). Your contractor should provide you a copy. Keep it permanently — it's required documentation if you sell the home, file an insurance claim, or apply for homeowner's insurance in Florida.

## Permit Timelines by Florida County

Standard permitting (3–10 business days): Orange, Hillsborough, Pinellas, Pasco, Polk, Duval, Sarasota, Manatee, Lee, Collier, Volusia, Brevard, Flagler, St. Johns, Alachua, Escambia, Leon.

Extended review (10–21 business days): Miami-Dade, Broward — due to mandatory HVHZ product review and NOA verification by the building department's plan review staff.

These timelines apply to over-the-counter or online permit submissions for standard residential re-roofs with no structural changes. Projects involving truss modifications, dormer additions, or solar panel integration require separate structural plan review and add 2–4 weeks.

## What Can Delay or Fail a Florida Roof Permit

**Failed final inspection** — The most common causes: wrong nail pattern for the wind zone, missing drip edge along the eaves (required statewide since the 2007 FBC update), improper flashing at walls and penetrations, or underlayment that doesn't match the approved spec sheet.

**Stop-work order** — Issued when work begins before permit approval or when the inspector finds work that doesn't match the permit drawings. Resolving a stop-work order typically adds 1–3 weeks to a project timeline and requires re-inspection fees ($75–$200 in most counties).

**Expired permit** — Florida building permits expire if work doesn't commence within 180 days of issuance, or if work is suspended for more than 180 days. An expired permit requires re-application and new fees.

**Unpermitted prior work** — If your home has unpermitted roofing from a previous owner, the building department may require retroactive correction before issuing a new permit. This is increasingly common in older neighborhoods in Tampa, St. Petersburg, and Jacksonville.

## Does a Permit Affect Your Homeowner's Insurance Rate?

It can — in the right direction. Florida insurers are required to offer discounts under the My Safe Florida Home program for homes with documentation of code-compliant roof installations. A valid Certificate of Completion is one of the qualifying documents. In some cases, a permitted roof replacement with wind mitigation features (hip roof geometry, secondary water barrier, clip or wraptie connectors) reduces the wind-portion premium by 20%–45% annually.

Have a licensed home inspector complete a wind mitigation inspection (OIR-B1-1802 form) within 90 days of your roof replacement. Submit it to your insurer with the CC for the maximum discount.
