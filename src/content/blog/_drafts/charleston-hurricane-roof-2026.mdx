# Charleston SC Hurricane Roof Prep: 2026 Season Guide

Charleston sits at the confluence of the Atlantic hurricane corridor and the Low Country's tidal flats — which means your roof faces salt-laden air 365 days a year and a realistic threat of Cat 1-3 wind loads every summer. The 2026 Atlantic hurricane season has already produced above-normal activity through the early months. August and September are the statistical peak, producing roughly 80% of named storm landfalls. If your roof isn't inspected and repaired before a named storm enters the forecast cone, you've likely missed your window.

This guide covers what Charleston homeowners specifically need to know — coastal wind codes, salt air corrosion, insurance traps, and how to time everything around evacuation orders.

## Why Charleston Roofs Age Faster Than Inland Homes

The Low Country's climate is harder on roofing materials than most homeowners realize. Salt air oxidizes metal flashing and fasteners faster than fresh-air environments — exposed galvanized nails near the coast show significant corrosion in as little as 5-7 years. Algae growth (those black streaks you see on shingles) is also accelerated by coastal humidity, and while algae looks cosmetic, it degrades asphalt shingles by trapping moisture against the surface.

Homes within 1,500 feet of saltwater — which includes large portions of James Island, Johns Island, Mount Pleasant, and the barrier islands — should use fasteners rated for coastal exposure (stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized to ASTM A153) and flashing products designed for marine environments. Standard aluminum flashing in a salt air environment can pit and fail within a decade.

The average shingle roof in coastal South Carolina lasts 15-20 years, compared to 20-25 years for the same product installed inland. If your roof is over 12 years old and hasn't been inspected since installation, budget time and money to find out exactly what you're dealing with before August.

## Charleston County Wind Load Requirements

Charleston County falls within South Carolina's coastal construction zone. Under ASCE 7-22 (adopted in the 2021 IBC and referenced in SC's current building code), residential structures in Charleston are designed for basic wind speeds of 130-140 mph depending on exact location and exposure category.

That translates to specific installation requirements:

- **Six nails per shingle** (not four, which is standard in lower wind zones)
- **Self-adhered secondary water barrier** (peel-and-stick underlayment covering the entire deck, not just the eave) for new roofs and full replacements in Exposure Category D
- **Drip edge** installed per IRC Section R905.2.8.5 — a detail frequently skipped by less experienced crews
- **Sealed roof deck** at all penetrations

If your home was re-roofed before 2009 or the work was done without a permit, there's a real possibility it doesn't meet current wind uplift standards. A licensed roofer can pull the permit history through the Charleston County Building Inspection Services Division and tell you what was installed and when.

## The Salt Air Inspection Checklist

Ground-level inspection catches about 60% of roof problems. The rest require getting eyes on flashing points and penetrations — which means either getting on the roof (not recommended without proper safety equipment) or hiring a contractor to do it. Here's what to look for from the ground and what to flag for a professional:

**Do yourself:**
- Walk the full perimeter with binoculars
- Look for shingles that are cupped, cracked, or missing
- Check gutters for granule accumulation (heavy granule loss = aging shingles)
- Look for rust streaks below any metal roof penetrations
- Go into the attic and check for daylight, soft sheathing, or rust-stained nail heads

**Requires a pro:**
- Flashing condition at chimney, skylights, pipe boots, and valleys
- Fastener corrosion assessment
- Drip edge seating and attachment
- Deck integrity in areas that appear soft or stained

## How Hurricane Storm Surge Differs From Wind Damage

Charleston's topography means storm surge is often more destructive than wind for properties below the 10-foot elevation mark — particularly in Zone A areas including Folly Beach, Sullivan's Island, Isle of Palms, and low-lying sections of West Ashley near the Ashley River.

Storm surge doesn't typically destroy roofs directly, but it creates interior flooding that wicks up wall assemblies into roof framing within hours. Once roof framing gets wet, mold begins in 24-48 hours under summer humidity conditions. If your attic has any existing moisture damage — visible on the sheathing as dark staining or soft spots — a surge event can trigger a full structural assessment.

Your roof prep should include verifying that attic soffit and ridge vents aren't open to water infiltration during wind-driven rain. Standard passive vents perform well in normal rain but can allow water entry when wind is coming at a 45-degree horizontal angle, as happens in squall bands.

## Step-by-Step: How to Prepare Your Charleston Roof for Hurricane Season

Complete these steps in order. The entire process should take no more than two weeks if you start now.

1. **Inspect the roof from ground level.** Walk the perimeter of your home and look for missing, curling, or cracked shingles. Use binoculars. Note any dark streaks (algae), sagging ridgelines, or granule accumulation in gutters — all signs of accelerated aging from salt air exposure.

2. **Check attic ventilation and interior for water stains.** Go into your attic during daylight. Look for daylight penetration through the deck, soft or discolored sheathing, and rust stains around nail heads. Any of these indicate active or prior water intrusion that will worsen under hurricane conditions.

3. **Clear gutters and downspouts completely.** Remove all debris from gutters and flush downspouts with a hose. Blocked gutters cause water to back up under drip edge during heavy rain bands — a common cause of interior ceiling damage during tropical systems.

4. **Photograph and document your roof's current condition.** Take date-stamped photos of the full roof surface, any visible damage, and all penetrations (vents, skylights, chimneys). Upload them to cloud storage. If you file a storm claim later, pre-storm documentation dramatically speeds up the adjuster's assessment.

5. **Have a licensed roofer inspect flashing, seals, and fasteners.** Hire a South Carolina licensed roofing contractor to inspect all flashing points — pipe boots, chimney flashing, valley metal, and drip edge. In coastal zones, salt air corrodes exposed metal fasteners and flashing within 5-7 years. Popped or corroded nails reduce uplift resistance significantly.

6. **Confirm your roof meets SC HVHZ or coastal wind requirements.** If your home was built before 2000 or re-roofed before the 2009 IRC updates, verify whether it meets current SC wind load requirements for your zone. Charleston County falls under ASCE 7-22 wind speeds of 130-140 mph for residential structures. Non-compliant roofs may require a secondary water barrier (self-adhered underlayment) to qualify for full insurance coverage.

7. **Complete all repairs at least two weeks before a named storm threatens.** Roofing contractors in the Charleston area book out 2-4 weeks during active hurricane season. Schedule and complete any repairs by early August at the latest. Work performed after a named storm is issued is rarely covered under standard insurance policies and is subject to contractor price gouging — illegal in SC under Code of Laws Section 39-5-145 but difficult to enforce during a crisis.

8. **Review your insurance policy for ACV vs. RCV coverage.** Pull your declarations page and confirm whether your policy pays Actual Cash Value (ACV) or Replacement Cost Value (RCV). ACV policies depreciate your roof's payout based on age — a 15-year-old roof may receive only 30-40% of replacement cost. If you have ACV coverage, talk to your agent about upgrading before storm season, not after.

9. **Plan your evacuation timeline around roof repair completion.** Charleston is under a mandatory evacuation zone system (Zones A through E). Zone A covers areas like Folly Beach, Isle of Palms, and Sullivan's Island. Once a Cat 2 or stronger storm enters the Gulf Stream corridor east of Florida, evacuations typically begin 48-72 hours out. Your roof work needs to be done well before that window closes.

If an inspection reveals your roof needs full replacement rather than targeted repairs, plan for these ranges on a standard Charleston single-family home:

- **Architectural asphalt shingles (GAF Timberline HDZ or equivalent):** $9,500-$15,000 for a 1,800-2,400 sq ft home, including coastal code compliance (six-nail pattern, self-adhered underlayment, stainless steel flashing)

- **Permit fees:** Charleston County residential roofing permits run $150-$400 depending on project valuation
- **Debris disposal:** Included in most contractor quotes, but verify before signing

Lead times for full replacements during active hurricane season run 3-6 weeks from contract signing to completion. If you're starting the process in late July, you're already at the edge of the safe window for a full replacement before peak season.

## Roofing Materials That Perform Best in Coastal SC Wind

Not all shingles are rated equally for wind. For Charleston's 130-140 mph design wind speeds, look for products carrying:

- **ASTM D3161 Class F** or **AC438** wind resistance ratings (Class F = 110 mph; AC438 = 130 mph)
- **ICC-ES ESR reports** confirming wind zone compliance at six-nail installation
- **UL 2218 Class 4 impact rating** — especially relevant for hail events that often accompany outer bands

GAF's Timberline HDZ qualifies under AC438 with six-nail application. CertainTeed's Landmark Pro carries similar ratings. If a contractor proposes a builder-grade shingle without verifying the wind rating for your specific zone, that's a red flag.

Metal roofing systems — particularly concealed-fastener standing seam — consistently outperform shingles in high-wind tests and carry manufacturer warranties that specifically cover wind speeds up to 140 mph when installed to spec. The higher upfront cost is partially offset by lower insurance premiums in many SC coastal policies.
